How this works

Where the data comes from

IMF PortWatch The IMF and the University of Oxford track ships by satellite (AIS signals) and publish, for each chokepoint, the number of ships passing each day and an estimate of the cargo they carry, split by vessel type. portwatch.imf.org

U.S. EIA Only in the “Energy (tankers)” view, the site adds the EIA's normal oil and LNG flows for the eight chokepoints it covers, pipeline bypass capacity, and oil estimates in barrels. World Oil Transit Chokepoints

All other calculations use PortWatch data only, so every cargo type (energy, dry bulk, containers and others) is measured the same way. World seaborne trade (12,720 million tonnes in 2024) comes from UNCTAD.

The chokepoint profiles are written summaries; every statement links to its source, such as the U.S. EIA, the IEA, UNCTAD or a canal authority.

Official updates Links to live notices from maritime security centres and canal authorities.

When the data updates

PortWatch publishes once a week, on Tuesdays, covering the previous week, so the latest day shown is usually 3 to 10 days old. No free source publishes reliable daily counts. This site checks for new data every day and also asks PortWatch directly each time the page opens.

What “normal” means

Every change compares the latest seven days with a reference period chosen at the top.

Seasonal normal (2019–2022) is the default: the same week averaged over the four years before the Red Sea crisis. It removes seasonal effects and is not distorted by one unusual year. It includes 2020, when the pandemic briefly reduced shipping.

Same week last year shows recent momentum, but misleads where last year was already disrupted.

Before the Iran war isolates the effect of the conflict that began on 28 February 2026.

Other major events, such as the Red Sea attacks or the Panama drought, appear as markers on each chokepoint's long-term chart rather than as extra comparison periods.

How the ranking works

Chokepoints are ranked by cargo missing per day: how many tonnes of cargo normally pass in a week like this one, minus how many passed in the latest week.

Cargo missing = normal cargo a day − latest cargo a day

It is measured in real units, so a large fall at a busy chokepoint ranks above the same percentage fall at a quiet one. Choosing a cargo type at the top ranks chokepoints by that type alone, for example dry bulk.

Cargo figures are PortWatch's estimates from ship-tracking data, not customs statistics.

Limits

Ships switch off tracking in conflict zones and satellite navigation is jammed, so falls in traffic may be overstated. Chokepoints with fewer than 5 ships a day normally are marked “low traffic”. The scenario calculator ignores stockpiles, prices and changes in demand. Not investment advice.

Abbreviations

mb/d: million barrels per day. bcf/d: billion cubic feet per day. LNG: liquefied natural gas. LPG: liquefied petroleum gas. Mt: million metric tons.
